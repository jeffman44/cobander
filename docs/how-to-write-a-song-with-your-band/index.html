How to write a song with your band

Writing alone is hard. Writing with three or four other opinions is harder — unless the band has a way of working. Here is one that works for garage bands and touring bands alike.

1. Start from one idea, not a blank page

Bands stall when everyone waits for inspiration together. Someone brings one thing — a riff, a chord loop, a chorus line, a drum feel — and the rest of the band reacts to it. One good idea plus four reactions beats four half-ideas fighting for the same space.

Keep a running list of these seeds. A riff from soundcheck, a line someone sang in the car: record it on anything, today, and give it a name you'll recognise later.

2. Settle the key and tempo early

Pick a tempo you can all play cleanly and a key the singer can actually reach at the top of the chorus. Changing either after everyone has learned their parts is painful. If the original key is too high, move it down a step or two before anyone gets attached.

3. Sketch the structure before you polish anything

Decide roughly where the verses, choruses and bridge go, and how many bars each one gets. A shared map stops the classic argument where half the band goes to the chorus and the other half plays the verse again. The song structure builder does this in a minute, and the templates guide covers the common forms.

4. Give every part an owner

The drummer owns the groove, the bass player owns the low end, guitars own the harmony and the hooks, the singer owns the melody and words. Owners make the call on their part after hearing the band's notes. Collective ownership of everything means nothing gets decided.

5. Write lyrics to a melody, not to a page

Sing nonsense syllables over the chords until a melody sticks, then fit words to it. Lyrics written first on paper often fight the rhythm of the music. Pin the final words to where they are sung so nobody is guessing which line goes in the second verse.

6. Work between practices, not only at practice

Rehearsal time is expensive — everyone in one room, the clock running. Use it for playing together and deciding. Do the rest at home: record a guitar idea, try a vocal line, leave a note on bar 17. When the band arrives, everyone has already heard the new parts.

7. Keep one version of the song

The fastest way to lose a good idea is "chorus_v3_FINAL_real.wav" sitting in five different group chats. Keep the song in one place that everyone can open, with the latest takes, chords, lyrics and notes together, and a history to go back to when an idea doesn't work out.

8. Know when it's done

Agree on what "finished" means before you start: all parts written, lyrics locked, a rough recording everyone approves. Without that, a band can tinker with one song for a year. Finish it, play it live, and fix it in the next one.

Questions

Who should write the lyrics in a band?

Usually the singer, because they have to sing them every night. Others can bring lines and ideas; the singer gets the final say.

How do we split songwriting credit?

Agree before the song is finished. Many bands split everything equally to avoid arguments; others split by who wrote the music and the words. Write the agreement down.

How long should it take to write a song?

There is no right answer, but setting a target — say, finished by the next show — keeps the band moving.
